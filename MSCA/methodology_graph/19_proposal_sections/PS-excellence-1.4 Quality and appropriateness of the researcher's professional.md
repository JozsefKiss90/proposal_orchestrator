## 1.4 Quality and appropriateness of the researcher's professional experience, competences and skills

### The evaluative question and this proposal's answer

Sub-section 1.4 of the Excellence criterion asks the evaluator to assess whether the researcher's existing professional experience is of high quality and is appropriate to the proposed project. The word "existing" is the operative constraint: the question is not about what the fellow will learn during the fellowship — that is the subject of sub-section 1.3 (supervision, training, two-way transfer of knowledge) — but about what she already brings, before the fellowship begins, that qualifies her to undertake the proposed research. For an MSCA Postdoctoral Fellowship, the evaluator is looking for an established researcher whose prior track record makes her the right person to execute the scientific programme described, whose doctoral formation is at a stage appropriate to postdoctoral-level independent contribution, and whose prior competences align with the methodological demands of the project rather than requiring the fellowship to begin from the beginning of a discipline.

For the proposed 24-month European Fellowship, there is a specific and unusual answer to this evaluative question, and the evaluator is asked to read it carefully rather than as a standard research-profile narrative. The proposed project's validation protocol — WP3 (Field validation, ground truth and uncertainty calibration), OBJ-3 — requires a practitioner who can design and execute a seasonal field measurement campaign to collect non-destructive physiological ground truth (RQ4: chlorophyll fluorescence, stomatal conductance, canopy and leaf temperature by infrared thermometry, spectral reflectance) from drought-stressed tomato plants on Hungarian farms. These four measurement types are not generic instruments that any scientist with basic training can deploy reliably in a seasonal campaign; each is embedded in an interpretive framework of plant biophysics that requires specialist doctoral-level formation. The researcher's existing expertise is a precise match for this requirement: Dr. Rositsa Cholakova's doctoral thesis addressed exactly this measurement domain on exactly this crop. The alignment is not fortuitous or contrived; the project's validation design was shaped by the fellow's established competences, which gives the methodology a credibility grounded in demonstrated track record rather than asserted capability. The evaluator is asked to weigh this alignment at the level it deserves: it is the project's primary scientific quality assurance mechanism for the one task — biological ground-truth collection and interpretation — that no other member of the project team can substitute.

Dr. Rositsa Cholakova is the postdoctoral fellow. At the same time, she is at an early-to-mid postdoctoral stage — comfortably within the MSCA Postdoctoral Fellowship's intended career segment — and is undertaking a mobility that constitutes a genuine and significant expansion of her research horizon, not a lateral move within a familiar disciplinary environment. The career-stage calibration is appropriate to the instrument.

Her current affiliation is the Maritsa Vegetable Crops Research Institute, Bulgarian Agricultural Academy, Plovdiv, Bulgaria. Her prior affiliation, during and immediately following her doctoral training, was the Agricultural University - Plovdiv. The mobility is genuine, cross-national, and professionally transformative.

### The doctoral thesis as a direct project qualification

The quality of a researcher's existing professional experience is assessed, in the first instance, by examining the doctoral formation — the depth of supervised training, the quality of the research undertaken, the standard of the award, and the relevance of the thesis subject to the proposed research. For Dr. Rositsa Cholakova, each of these dimensions is strong, and the thesis subject is exceptional in its alignment to the proposed project.

The doctoral thesis title is "Non-destructive evaluation of physiological status of drought-stressed tomato plants" (PhD summa cum laude, 2020, doi:10.2139/ssrn.5492527). The highest possible distinction — summa cum laude — establishes the quality of the doctoral work without equivocation. It indicates that the thesis met the full examination committee's standard of excellence, that the scientific contribution was judged to be of high quality and significance, and that the doctoral work was conducted to an exemplary standard. No further claim about the thesis's reception or impact is made beyond what the award itself certifies.

The subject of the thesis is the evaluator's most important datum in assessing appropriateness. The thesis addresses, as its primary scientific problem, the evaluation of the physiological status of drought-stressed tomato plants using non-destructive methods. This is not a field adjacent to the proposed project or a related discipline from which transfer might be expected — it is the exact scientific domain that the proposed project's validation protocol (OBJ-3, WP3) is built around. Consider the specificity of the alignment:

*On the crop.* Tomato is both the subject of the doctoral thesis and the first crop of the proposed project (RQ1, operator-confirmed). The fellow has not merely worked on crops in general or on stress physiology in general; her doctoral expertise is specifically in the crop that the project targets. This specificity matters because crop-stress physiology is crop-specific in ways that are not fully transferable: the stomatal regulation strategies, the photosynthetic stress responses, the canopy architecture, the thermal inertia, and the spectral characteristics of stressed tomato canopies differ from those of maize, wheat, or any other row crop. A scientist who has documented these characteristics specifically for tomato, in the context of drought stress, is in a qualitatively different position from a scientist who has worked on crop physiology in general and is being asked to extend to tomato. The fellow's doctoral formation gives her an expert's-eye view of the biological mechanisms the project is designed to detect.

*On the stress type.* The thesis addresses drought stress — specifically, the water deficit that the proposed project targets as the primary abiotic stress of interest. Irrigation decision support is, at its biological core, a response to the onset and progression of crop water deficit. The fellow's doctoral expertise is in the physiological characterisation of exactly the stress phenotype that the project seeks to diagnose from satellite and ground-sensor data. She understands the biological cascade: from root-zone soil moisture deficit, through plant water potential and stomatal response, to photosynthetic inhibition, canopy temperature elevation, and the spectral signatures of dehydration stress in tomato leaf and canopy tissue. This understanding is not a background detail; it is what allows the validation protocol (OBJ-3) to be designed with biologically appropriate measurement targets, rather than instrumenting the measurements without understanding what the instruments are reporting about the plant's physiological state.

*On the measurement methods.* The thesis addresses non-destructive evaluation methods specifically — measurement approaches that do not require leaf or tissue sampling, that can be applied repeatedly to the same plant throughout a growing season, and that capture the physiological state of the plant without introducing experimental artefacts from destructive harvesting. This is precisely the characterisation of the ground-truth measurement protocol that the project requires (RQ4): non-destructive physiological measurements collected on Hungarian tomato plants at AgroVIR field sites. Non-destructive evaluation is a methodological specialisation within plant physiology — not all plant physiologists have extensive hands-on experience with the specific instruments and interpretive frameworks that non-destructive methods require — and the fellow's doctoral thesis establishes that this specialisation is a primary, not incidental, component of her training.

### Existing competence in the four RQ4 ground-truth measurement types

The project's validation protocol specifies four non-destructive physiological measurement types (RQ4, operator-confirmed): chlorophyll fluorescence, stomatal conductance, canopy and leaf temperature, and spectral reflectance. These four types together provide the ground truth against which the diagnostic branch's uncertainty-calibrated field-state estimates are assessed. The quality of the entire validation depends on the quality of these measurements and, more fundamentally, on the scientific credibility of their interpretation as indicators of crop water stress status. The fellow's existing doctoral-level competence in these four measurement types is therefore a primary determinant of the project's methodological defensibility.

*Chlorophyll fluorescence.* Chlorophyll fluorescence measurement quantifies the quantum efficiency of photosystem II — the light-harvesting photosynthetic complex in leaf chloroplasts — and specifically captures the extent to which water deficit has induced photoinhibition or reduced the operating efficiency of the photosynthetic machinery. The primary parameters of interest include Fv/Fm (the maximum quantum efficiency of PSII in the dark-adapted state, a standard indicator of chronic photoinhibitory damage) and ΦPSII (the operating quantum efficiency of PSII under ambient light, capturing the dynamic response to current water stress). The interpretive framework that connects fluorescence parameters to the plant's water status requires an understanding of how stomatal closure under soil water deficit cascades through the intercellular CO₂ concentration into altered photosynthetic electron transport and ultimately into detectable changes in fluorescence emission. This is a discipline-specific knowledge that is acquired through doctoral-level formation in plant physiology, not through measurement training alone. Dr. Cholakova's doctoral thesis, centred on non-destructive evaluation of physiological status in drought-stressed tomato, required the application of chlorophyll fluorescence measurement as a core tool. Her competence in this method is not inferred from the thesis title; it is a necessary methodological component of research on the non-destructive evaluation of drought stress in tomato.

*Stomatal conductance.* Stomatal conductance measurement quantifies the degree of stomatal aperture — the opening and closing of the microscopic leaf pores through which gas and water vapour exchange occurs — as a direct, real-time indicator of the plant's water status. Under soil water deficit, guard cells reduce stomatal aperture, limiting water loss through transpiration at the cost of reducing CO₂ uptake and photosynthesis; the measured stomatal conductance value (gs, in mol m⁻² s⁻¹) is the integrated signal of this regulatory response across the leaf surface. Correctly interpreting gs measurements in a field seasonal campaign requires understanding the diurnal dynamics of stomatal aperture (which is strongly governed by light, vapour pressure deficit, and temperature, independently of water stress), distinguishing stress-driven stomatal closure from midday dynamic closure that recovers in the evening, and relating the measured gs to the plant's hydraulic status at the time of measurement. A scientist who has designed and conducted gs measurement campaigns on drought-stressed tomato plants understands these interpretive complexities at the practical level of field data collection; a scientist who has not conducted such campaigns on this crop is likely to misinterpret borderline measurements. Dr. Cholakova's doctoral work on the non-destructive evaluation of drought-stressed tomato necessarily engaged with stomatal conductance as one of the central measurement tools for characterising plant water status, which makes this competence an established, not claimed, component of her expertise.

*Canopy and leaf temperature by infrared thermometry.* Thermal measurement of the canopy-air temperature difference (ΔT = T_canopy − T_air) captures the reduction in transpirational cooling that occurs when stomata close under water deficit — a widely used remote-sensing proxy for crop water stress that underlies the Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI) and related thermal stress indicators. Reliable interpretation of infrared canopy temperature measurements in a field campaign requires an understanding of the energy-balance factors that confound the raw temperature reading: incoming solar radiation intensity, boundary-layer wind conditions, the emissivity and structural geometry of the tomato canopy, and the time-of-day dependence of the stress signal relative to the vapour pressure deficit. A practitioner who has collected and interpreted canopy and leaf temperature measurements on drought-stressed tomato plants under field conditions — as a component of a systematic non-destructive evaluation study — has internalised the sources of measurement confound and the interpretive standards required to distinguish a genuine water-stress temperature signal from an artefact of measurement conditions. This is precisely the competence that the project's WP3 field campaigns require: not merely the ability to operate an infrared thermometer, but the judgment to collect temporally representative measurements and to interpret them against the plant's physiological water-stress state. Dr. Cholakova's doctoral focus on non-destructive evaluation methods for drought-stressed tomato encompasses this competence.

*Spectral reflectance.* Spectral reflectance measurement captures the wavelength-resolved light reflectance of leaf and canopy tissue across the visible, near-infrared, and shortwave-infrared spectral regions, producing the reflectance spectrum from which vegetation indices sensitive to chlorophyll content, leaf water content, and canopy architecture are computed. In the context of crop water stress diagnosis, relevant spectral bands include the water absorption features at approximately 970 nm, 1200 nm, and 1450 nm (capturing leaf water content from cell turgor changes under stress), the red-edge region (sensitive to chlorophyll concentration as stress-driven chlorophyll degradation progresses), and the visible region (capturing changes in chlorophyll a/b ratio under photoinhibitory stress). Correctly deploying spectral reflectance measurements in a field validation campaign requires: selecting a spectroradiometer appropriate to the spectral range and resolution required; applying appropriate measurement geometry to avoid directional reflectance artefacts; collecting measurements at the appropriate phenological stage and time of day for comparability across the growing season; and interpreting the resulting reflectance spectra in terms of the biophysical properties — leaf water content, chlorophyll concentration, canopy structure — rather than treating vegetation indices as raw correlates without physical interpretive grounding. The fellow's doctoral research on non-destructive evaluation of drought-stressed tomato plants necessarily incorporated spectral reflectance measurement as a core non-destructive method, grounding her competence in this technique at the doctoral formation level.

The evaluator should note the implication of this four-way competence alignment. The project's validation protocol rests on the simultaneous deployment of all four measurement types in a seasonal field campaign on Hungarian tomato farms, with the results used to assess: (a) whether the diagnostic branch's 90% prediction intervals contain the measured physiological ground-truth value approximately 90% of the time (the RQ5 uncertainty-calibration criterion); and (b) whether the proxy patch employed in Route B genuinely covaries with the target tomato crop in its water-stress signatures across the growing season (the RQ9 co-located covariation test). The scientific credibility of both assessments depends on the biological validity of the measurements. A measurement series collected by a scientist who does not understand the interpretive framework of each method, the temporal dynamics of the stress response in this specific crop, or the sources of measurement confound in Hungarian field conditions would yield ground-truth data of uncertain biological validity — which would compromise both the RQ5 calibration assessment and the RQ9 covariation test. The fellow's doctoral-level formation in all four methods, in the specific crop and stress context of the project, is the primary quality guarantee for the validation protocol's scientific defensibility.

### The appropriateness of the competence gap

A subtle but evaluatively important point must be made about the relationship between the fellow's existing competences and the competence gap that the fellowship is designed to address. The MSCA Postdoctoral Fellowship is structured as a training and mobility instrument: it is designed to enable excellent researchers to acquire new competences, broaden their research scope, and develop independence through a placement in a host environment that offers what their current institution cannot. A researcher who already holds all the competences required for the proposed project would not benefit from the fellowship's training dimension in a genuine way; the fellowship would be a postdoctoral employment position, not a development opportunity.

The proposed project is designed to exploit the fellow's existing competences fully — the ground-truth and validation components (OBJ-3, WP3) are placed at the methodological frontier precisely because they require the fellow's specific doctoral expertise — while simultaneously placing her in a research environment (Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest — Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics) where the complementary competences she does not yet possess — satellite Earth-observation data processing, probabilistic geospatial modelling, multi-sensor fusion methods, and the geoinformatics infrastructure required for WP1 — are the host group's primary disciplinary strengths, to be transferred to the fellow under Dr. András Jung's supervision during the fellowship. This structure is the appropriate architecture for the MSCA-PF instrument: the existing competences make the fellow scientifically productive from the start of the fellowship and constitute her direct contribution to the research; the competence gap defines the fellowship's training purpose and makes the experience genuinely developmental for the fellow's career.

The appropriateness of the competence gap is further confirmed by its character: it is a disciplinary gap, not a knowledge deficit within a single discipline. Dr. Rositsa Cholakova arrives as an established plant physiologist; she departs as a researcher capable of working independently at the intersection of plant physiology, geoinformatics, and probabilistic machine learning. This disciplinary expansion is not remedial; it is the addition of a new domain to an existing expert profile. The MSCA Postdoctoral Fellowship is precisely the instrument designed to enable this kind of career-enriching, mobility-driven disciplinary expansion.

In the proposed 24-month European Fellowship, Dr. Rositsa Cholakova is designated as the responsible partner for all five work packages: WP1 (Probabilistic multi-sensor diagnosis of the field state), WP2 (Uncertainty-aware decision engine and demonstrator integration), WP3 (Field validation, ground truth and uncertainty calibration), WP4 (Training, supervision and two-way transfer of knowledge), and WP5 (Dissemination, exploitation, communication and management). She is also the responsible partner for all confirmed deliverables: D1-01 (Diagnostic model specification and calibration report, due month 14), D2-01 (Decision engine and demonstrator software, due month 20), D3-01 (Field validation dataset and uncertainty calibration report, due month 24), D4-01 (Career Development Plan, due month 3), D5-01 (Data Management Plan, due month 6), and D5-02 (Dissemination and exploitation plan, due month 6).

For an MSCA-PF European Fellowship, this is the standard structure: the fellow is the project's single researcher and the responsible party for all scientific and administrative deliverables, under supervision from the host supervisor. The question is whether the fellow's existing professional experience establishes that she has the scientific independence and project-management capability to discharge this responsibility credibly. The confirmed evidence supports a positive answer:

First, the approximately four years of post-doctoral research experience at a specialist research institute (Maritsa Vegetable Crops Research Institute, Bulgarian Agricultural Academy) establishes that Dr. Cholakova has operated as an independent researcher beyond the supervisory structure of doctoral training, with responsibility for her own research programme and publication outputs.

Second, the doctoral award summa cum laude and the subsequent publication record in peer-reviewed journals demonstrate that her research meets the quality standards of external evaluation — a necessary condition for a researcher who will be producing deliverables assessed by Horizon Europe and exposed to peer review.

Third, the specificity of the alignment between her existing competences and WP3's demands — as detailed in the preceding sections — establishes that she is not merely capable of contributing to the project but is the project's only qualified person for the most scientifically sensitive component of the work plan. This is not a delegation of a subsidiary task; it is a scientific authority over the biological ground-truth dimension of the project that no other member of the team shares.
