## 1.3 Quality of the supervision, training and of the two-way transfer of knowledge between the researcher and the host

### The host institution and its disciplinary environment

The host beneficiary for this 24-month European Fellowship is Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest — Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics, Budapest, Hungary. ELTE is Hungary's leading research university and, as a higher education establishment, holds the institutional status, international experience, and administrative infrastructure required to host internationally mobile researchers under the Horizon Europe framework. The specific hosting unit — the Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics — concentrates its research in geoinformatics and remote sensing. This disciplinary concentration is the primary scientific rationale for the fellowship's location: geoinformatics and multi-sensor Earth observation are the methodological domain that supplies WP1 (Probabilistic multi-sensor diagnosis of the field state) with its satellite data processing pipelines, hyperspectral imaging and field spectroscopy techniques, spatial data harmonisation procedures, and multisensor-drone fusion methods. These are capabilities that the fellow, Dr. Rositsa Cholakova, arrives without — she comes from the Maritsa Vegetable Crops Research Institute, Bulgarian Agricultural Academy, a plant-physiology institute that does not operate at the scale of satellite multi-source data processing and probabilistic geospatial modelling — and that she can only acquire by working embedded in an active geoinformatics research group.

As a Hungarian public university and Horizon Europe beneficiary, Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest operates under the principles of the European Charter for Researchers and the Code of Conduct for the Recruitment of Researchers, which govern the working conditions, contractual frameworks, intellectual property arrangements, and career support structures applicable to Dr. Rositsa Cholakova throughout the fellowship. This institutional governance serves expected outcome EO-04 (increased alignment of working conditions for researchers in accordance with the principles set out in the European Charter for Researchers) and expected impact EI-05 (increased alignment of working conditions for researchers in accordance with the principles set out in the European Charter for Researchers, contributing to more equitable and attractive research careers across Europe). The evaluator should note that the confirmation of ELTE's institutional type as a higher education establishment — a type with legal obligations under both the Charter and Hungary's implementation of Horizon Europe participation rules — is the grounding for these outcome and impact claims, not a generic assertion.

### Qualifications and experience of the supervisor

The supervisor is Dr. András Jung, Deputy Head of the Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics at Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest — Faculty of Informatics. His scientific expertise spans remote sensing, hyperspectral imaging and field spectroscopy, and multisensor-drone fusion. He is Head of the Multisensor-Drone-Fusion Working Group at ELTE (project ELTE TKP2021-NVA-29), which operates the multi-sensor observation infrastructure and the associated research programme that is directly relevant to the diagnostic branch of this project. His leadership of an active research working group at this level demonstrates a sustained track record of directing research at the advanced level required for postdoctoral supervision under the MSCA-PF instrument.

Dr. András Jung holds habilitations in both Computer Science and Crop Sciences. This dual disciplinary qualification is directly relevant to the supervision assignment: the fellowship sits at the intersection of geoinformatics and plant-crop science, and a supervisor who is credentialled in both parent disciplines is positioned to provide meaningful oversight of the project's full interdisciplinary scope — including the AquaCrop crop model calibration in WP2 (Uncertainty-aware decision engine and demonstrator integration), the biological interpretation of crop water stress status in the diagnostic model, and the agronomic reasoning behind the water-use-efficiency decision objective (RQ6) — rather than providing authoritative geoinformatics supervision while treating the crop-science content as an opaque black box contributed by the fellow. The Crop Sciences habilitation is the formal qualification that gives Dr. Jung the standing to supervise the project's plant-physiological and agronomical content coherently, enabling substantive intellectual exchange with the fellow on the scientific content of her doctoral expertise rather than relying on her judgment alone for those components.

Dr. András Jung is co-founder of Cubert GmbH, a manufacturer of hyperspectral snapshot cameras, and of Brightic Research. These entrepreneurial activities provide a direct, experiential track record in translating spectroscopic and hyperspectral research methods from academic proof of concept to commercial instruments and applied research services. This track record has two distinct supervisory implications for this fellowship. First, it constitutes first-hand mentorship capacity in the knowledge valorisation and innovation/entrepreneurship dimension of the MSCA-PF mandatory training requirements (scope requirement SR-03), supplying the fellow with supervision grounded in the practical experience of moving a research method through the TRL ladder — directly analogous to the trajectory the project's standalone research demonstrator (OBJ-4, D2-01, Decision engine and demonstrator software, due month 20) would follow from TRL 3–4 toward potential future uptake. Second, Dr. Jung's experience as a researcher who has directly engaged with the commercial translation of academic instrumentation provides the practical knowledge of intellectual property considerations, valorisation routes, and stakeholder engagement with technology users that the fellow's training in this dimension requires. A supervision arrangement in which this training dimension is provided by someone who has actually co-founded a commercial spin-out from spectroscopic research is qualitatively different from one in which it is provided by a researcher with no such direct experience.

The supervision pairing is scientifically asymmetric in a complementary way that is distinctive within the MSCA-PF instrument's logic. Dr. András Jung brings the geoinformatics, multi-sensor Earth-observation methodology, hyperspectral imaging and field spectroscopy infrastructure, and translational experience; Dr. Rositsa Cholakova brings the plant physiology and non-destructive crop stress phenotyping competence. Neither is a junior contributor deferring to the senior party's expertise across the full scope of the project; each is the primary scientific authority for a different but functionally connected component of the work. This asymmetric complementarity defines a supervisory relationship that is genuinely mentoring — the supervisor extends the fellow's methodological horizon in a direction she cannot access independently — while also being scientifically mutual: the fellow's contribution is load-bearing for the project's success, not auxiliary to the supervisor's own research programme.

### Supervision structure and Career Development Plan

Supervision is structured around the Career Development Plan (D4-01, Career Development Plan, due month 3), jointly prepared by Dr. András Jung and Dr. Rositsa Cholakova and submitted as a confirmed project deliverable at the beginning of the action, as required by scope requirement SR-04. The month 3 submission deadline is not a bureaucratic convention; it ensures that the jointly agreed targets for research milestones, training activities, dissemination, and career development are documented and committed before the project's intensive data-collection and probabilistic modelling phases begin in earnest. A CDP submitted at the beginning of the fellowship governs the supervision throughout its duration; a CDP produced retrospectively would be an audit record, not a planning instrument.

The Career Development Plan is a living instrument, not a one-time filing. It is reviewed and updated during the 24-month fellowship. The longitudinal review structure is the mechanism through which supervision quality and research-training sustainability — expected outcome EO-05 (enhanced quality and sustainability of research training and supervision) — are served in practice rather than merely committed in principle: a review cycle that tracks the fellow's actual progress against the CDP's targets, and revises training priorities and career-development planning as the research evolves and the fellow's competence development through the fellowship becomes clearer, is what distinguishes genuinely adaptive supervision from compliance with a submission requirement.

The CDP covers the following domains, each grounded in the project's confirmed architecture and the supervisor's confirmed competences: the fellow's research objectives and their relationship to the work-plan milestones (OBJ-1 through OBJ-5, across WP1 through WP5); training and career development needs across all five MSCA-PF mandatory transferable-skills dimensions specified in scope requirement SR-03 (digital and AI skills including the responsible use of generative AI; knowledge valorisation; innovation and entrepreneurship; research integrity; and open science/FAIR data stewardship); publication and open-science activity planning; and the two-way transfer of knowledge programme with its methods-exchange milestones. Specific quantitative targets for training activities and career outputs — hours of training per dimension, publication counts, seminar counts, thesis co-supervision commitments — are determined jointly between supervisor and fellow in the CDP process itself, as the instrument requires (SR-04). They are not pre-asserted in this proposal: the MSCA-PF instrument designates the CDP as the appropriate instrument for jointly setting these targets, and asserting specific quantitative commitments in the proposal without the supervisor's joint input in the CDP process would misrepresent the collaborative structure the instrument mandates.

Regular research reviews are conducted under Dr. András Jung's supervision within the host's geoinformatics and remote-sensing group throughout the 24-month fellowship. These reviews serve the early identification of methodological risks and adaptive revision of priorities within the pre-committed two-route architecture: notably the risk that Route B's patch-covariation assumption fails the formal seasonal co-location test (RQ9), which would trigger the pre-committed shift to Route A as the primary diagnostic path, and the risk that the diagnostic branch's posterior estimates fail the uncertainty-calibration criterion (RQ5: 90% prediction intervals must contain the measured ground-truth value approximately 90% of the time on the holdout validation set), which would trigger post-calibration correction. The supervisory review structure is therefore calibrated to the project's own risk management design: the supervisor is the scientifically most qualified person to assess the multi-sensor EO diagnostic branch's methodological adequacy at each review point, which means that his regular supervisory engagement with the diagnostic branch also constitutes the project's early-warning system for its most scientifically consequential risks.

WP4 (Training, supervision and two-way transfer of knowledge) is led by Dr. András Jung and spans the full 24-month fellowship (months 1–24), ensuring that supervision and training are continuous throughout the action and not front-loaded into the CDP preparation period alone.

### Two-way transfer of knowledge between researcher and host (European Fellowship)

This fellowship is a European Fellowship; the applicable sub-section requirement is the two-way transfer of knowledge between researcher and host. The following account makes the case that the transfer is bidirectional by scientific necessity — not as a formal arrangement designed to satisfy the application form criterion — and that both directions of the transfer are load-bearing for the project's execution.

**The rationale for bidirectionality.** The scientific problem the project addresses is constituted at the intersection of two distinct research disciplines that do not ordinarily operate together: plant physiology and non-destructive crop stress phenotyping on one side, and geoinformatics and multi-sensor Earth observation on the other. The end-to-end uncertainty-aware framework the project develops — from multi-sensor satellite observation through probabilistic latent field-state diagnosis to uncertainty-weighted irrigation decision — requires both disciplines simultaneously and at the level of their respective methodological depth. No individual researcher, and no single research group, holds the full combination of competences required to design, execute, and validate the proposed framework. The two-way transfer of knowledge is the mechanism through which the project assembles the missing competence on each side. The transfer is genuine because it is scientifically required; it would be necessary even if the MSCA-PF instrument did not ask for it.

**Fellow to host: plant physiology and non-destructive stress phenotyping into the geoinformatics group.** Dr. Rositsa Cholakova brings to the Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest — Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics group a practitioner-level expertise in plant physiology and the non-destructive evaluation of crop water and abiotic stress that the host group does not hold as an internal scientific capability. This expertise is constitutively required for WP3 (Field validation, ground truth and uncertainty calibration) and for the project's OBJ-3 (Field validation and honest uncertainty calibration). The four non-destructive physiological measurement types that constitute the project's ground-truth evidence base (RQ4, operator-confirmed) — chlorophyll fluorescence, stomatal conductance, canopy and leaf temperature, and spectral reflectance — are not generic measurement instruments that any scientist with basic training can deploy reliably in a seasonal field campaign; each embeds an interpretive framework of plant biophysics that requires discipline-specific competence to apply correctly and to interpret against the crop's physiological water-stress status.

Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements quantify the quantum efficiency of photosystem II and detect water-stress-induced photoinhibition through specific fluorescence parameters; their correct interpretation requires familiarity with the relationship between the fluorescence parameters and the photosynthetic machinery's response to water deficit. Stomatal conductance measurements quantify the degree of stomatal closure as a regulatory response to plant water deficit through a measurement that integrates mesophyll water status and vapour-pressure gradient, and whose interpretation requires knowledge of the stomatal regulation mechanisms that govern the gas-exchange-water-use tradeoff. Canopy and leaf temperature measurements by infrared thermometry capture the canopy-air temperature difference that arises under water stress through reduced transpirational cooling, and their reliable interpretation requires the ability to distinguish this stress-driven temperature difference from confounding influences of boundary-layer conditions and incoming solar radiation. Spectral reflectance measurements capture vegetation indices and water-sensitive spectral features whose interpretation requires knowledge of which spectral regions respond to leaf water content, chlorophyll, and canopy architecture, and how the ratio of these influences shifts across the stress gradient.

Designing a seasonal measurement campaign on Hungarian tomato farms that captures the correct phenological window for water stress expression, distinguishes water-deficit symptoms from nutrient deficiency and other stress signals in field conditions, and provides a measurement record credible enough to assess the 90% prediction-interval coverage criterion (RQ5) against — all of this requires a scientist who understands the measurement instruments, the plant biophysics behind them, and the tomato crop's specific stress response characteristics. Dr. Rositsa Cholakova's doctoral thesis addressed the non-destructive evaluation of the physiological status of drought-stressed tomato plants (PhD summa cum laude, 2020, doi:10.2139/ssrn.5492527), making her the disciplinary authority for the exact measurement domain and the exact crop that the project's validation protocol targets. Her expertise is not claimed generically; it is demonstrated in the subject matter of her most significant research output and in the match between that subject matter and the project's validation design.

Through the fellowship, the fellow's plant-physiology and non-destructive stress-phenotyping expertise is transferred into the host's geoinformatics group. By the end of the fellowship, Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest — Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics will have acquired, through the evidence produced in D3-01 (Field validation dataset and uncertainty calibration report, due month 24), a validated methodology for conducting biologically credible field campaigns for crop-water-stress monitoring — campaigns that interpret remote-sensing proxies against direct plant-physiological measurements rather than treating biological grounding as assumed. This capacity did not exist in the host group before the fellowship; its acquisition is a durable strengthening of the host's R&I capacity within the meaning of expected outcome EO-07 (stronger R&I capacity and output among participating organisations; better transfer of knowledge) and expected impact EI-03 (stronger R&I capacity and output among participating organisations and better transfer of knowledge between participating organisations).

**Host to fellow: geoinformatics and multi-sensor Earth observation into the plant physiology researcher.** The Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest — Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics, under Dr. András Jung's scientific leadership, transfers to Dr. Rositsa Cholakova the methods and working environment of a mature geoinformatics and multi-sensor Earth-observation research group. The specific competences and resources transferred include: satellite Earth-observation data acquisition and quality-control pipelines for Sentinel-1 SAR radar backscatter (all-weather soil-moisture-sensitive acquisition), Sentinel-2 optical surface reflectance (vegetation-state-sensitive multispectral acquisition), and PlanetScope high-resolution imagery (access confirmed via Planet platform and API, RQ8, operator-confirmed); hyperspectral imaging and field spectroscopy practices developed through Dr. Jung's research group and directly connected to the Cubert GmbH hyperspectral camera technology; spatial data harmonisation procedures for combining data streams of different spatial resolution (3 m to ~9 km), temporal cadence (daily to 5–12 day revisit), and acquisition geometry (SAR versus optical) — the technical challenge addressed in WP1, Task T1-01; multisensor-drone fusion techniques from the Multisensor-Drone-Fusion Working Group (project ELTE TKP2021-NVA-29) that complement the satellite EO pipeline; and the geospatial data management infrastructure — computing environment, geospatial processing platform, data storage and retrieval systems — that the project's multi-sensor time series requires for processing at scale over Hungarian field boundaries across multiple growing seasons.

None of these methodological tools and resources is available at the Maritsa Vegetable Crops Research Institute, Bulgarian Agricultural Academy, where Dr. Rositsa Cholakova is currently based. Her current institute operates at the scale of crop science and laboratory measurement; it has not operated a satellite multi-sensor data processing pipeline, and it does not maintain the geospatial computing infrastructure that WP1 requires. The host's geoinformatics competences are therefore entirely new to the fellow and not competences she brings with her. The host-to-fellow transfer is the mechanism through which Dr. Rositsa Cholakova acquires, through daily working practice in the group, through supervision by Dr. András Jung, and through the execution of the diagnostic and decision tasks in WP1 (Probabilistic multi-sensor diagnosis of the field state) and WP2 (Uncertainty-aware decision engine and demonstrator integration), the capacity to process multi-sensor satellite data, apply probabilistic machine-learning models to latent field-state inference, calibrate crop models in an uncertainty-aware way, and manage geospatial data at scale.

By the end of the 24-month fellowship, the combination of her original plant-physiology and non-destructive stress-phenotyping expertise with the geoinformatics and probabilistic Earth-observation competences acquired through the transfer constitutes a research profile — a scientist capable of independently designing and executing an end-to-end pipeline from multi-sensor satellite observation through probabilistic field-state diagnosis to uncertainty-aware irrigation decision, validated against plant-physiological ground truth — that is not achievable by a plant physiologist who has not received this transfer and not achievable by a geoinformatician who lacks the biological validation expertise she brings. This unique combined profile advances Dr. Rositsa Cholakova's employability and career prospects within academia and beyond in a manner that directly serves expected outcome EO-01 (increased set of research and transferable skills and competences, leading to improved employability and career prospects of MSCA postdoctoral fellows within academia and beyond) and expected impact EI-01 (improved employability and career prospects of MSCA postdoctoral fellows within academia and beyond, contributing to a more mobile and highly skilled European research workforce).

**Mutual irreplaceability.** The two directions of transfer are mutually irreplaceable within the project's scientific framework. The host cannot credibly execute WP3 (Field validation, ground truth and uncertainty calibration) without the fellow's plant-physiological measurement expertise: the ground-truth protocol (RQ4) requires a scientist who understands plant water relations, not only a scientist who can process satellite data. The fellow cannot credibly execute WP1 (Probabilistic multi-sensor diagnosis of the field state) without the host's EO data infrastructure, geospatial computing environment, and multi-sensor fusion supervision. The project's primary science deliverable — the formalised in D1-01 (Diagnostic model specification and calibration report, due month 14) — and its validation deliverable — formalised in D3-01 (Field validation dataset and uncertainty calibration report, due month 24) — are each the product of both directions of transfer working simultaneously. Neither deliverable can be produced by one party alone. This mutual irreplaceability is the scientific justification for the specific researcher-host pairing and is the most direct evidence that the two-way transfer of knowledge is genuine rather than formal.

**Planned evidence and milestones for the transfer.** The two-way transfer is planned to be evidenced through the project's deliverable structure and through the Career Development Plan (D4-01, due month 3) rather than through ad hoc activities appended to the work plan. The fellow's contribution of plant-physiology expertise to the host group is evidenced through the seasonal field measurement campaigns of WP3, culminating in D3-01 (Field validation dataset and uncertainty calibration report, due month 24). The host group's contribution of geoinformatics methods to the fellow's competence development is evidenced through the EO data pipeline and diagnostic model produced in WP1, formalised in D1-01 (Diagnostic model specification and calibration report, due month 14). Methods seminars and knowledge exchange events through which the fellow formally presents plant-physiology and non-destructive measurement methods to the host group's geoinformatics researchers are planned within WP4 (Training, supervision and two-way transfer of knowledge) and will be specified in the CDP; specific event counts are not pre-asserted here and are determined in the CDP process. These exchanges directly serve expected outcome EO-02 (new mind-sets and approaches to R&I work forged through international, inter-sectoral and interdisciplinary experience) and expected outcome EO-08 (regular feedback of research results into teaching and education at participating organisations), since seminars delivered within the host's research group embed the project's methodology in the group's collective scientific practice and, through the host's educational structure, in its teaching activities.

### Planned training activities for the researcher

WP4 (Training, supervision and two-way transfer of knowledge), led by Dr. András Jung and spanning months 1–24, delivers structured training to Dr. Rositsa Cholakova across all five mandatory transferable-skills dimensions specified in scope requirement SR-03 of call HORIZON-MSCA-2026-PF-01. The Career Development Plan (D4-01, due month 3) is the governing instrument for training targets, and specific quantitative targets per dimension are determined therein jointly by supervisor and fellow. The following account addresses the qualitative content of each dimension, grounded in the project's confirmed architecture and the supervisor's confirmed competences.

**Scientific training through the research programme.** The most substantial component of the fellow's scientific training is the execution of the research work plan itself. Working in the host's geoinformatics and remote-sensing group under Dr. András Jung's supervision across WP1, WP2, and WP3, Dr. Rositsa Cholakova develops competences in: satellite Earth-observation data acquisition and quality control for multi-source agricultural monitoring; probabilistic machine-learning model design and posterior inference for latent-variable estimation from noisy multi-sensor data (Bayesian hierarchical models and Gaussian processes); spatial data harmonisation across sensors of different resolution, cadence, and acquisition geometry; uncertainty-aware calibration of the FAO AquaCrop crop model through retention of a parameter posterior; counterfactual simulation of irrigation decisions over weather ensembles; scoring of candidate decisions by expected water-use efficiency under joint field-state and weather uncertainty; and field-scale geospatial data management for multi-source time series. These scientific competences are transferred to the fellow through the host's disciplinary environment and Dr. Jung's direct supervision, at the methodological frontier of multi-sensor Earth observation and probabilistic agricultural modelling. In parallel, Dr. Rositsa Cholakova continues to develop her plant-physiology and non-destructive measurement competences through the execution of WP3 (Field validation, ground truth and uncertainty calibration), deepening her experience with field-scale physiological measurement campaigns in Hungarian agricultural conditions and with the interpretation of non-destructive measurements against the uncertainty-calibration criterion (RQ5) that the project's validation design requires. The combination of research-domain extension (geoinformatics added to existing plant physiology) and depth-of-practice development (validation protocol design and execution at scale) constitutes the scientific training dimension of the fellowship.

**Digital and AI skills, including the responsible use of generative AI.** The project's scientific core is probabilistic machine learning and uncertainty quantification, making the digital and AI skills dimension inseparable from the scientific training. The training covers: design and implementation of probabilistic models (Bayesian hierarchical models, Gaussian processes) in scientific computing environments for the diagnostic branch of WP1; Bayesian posterior inference algorithms and their practical implementation; uncertainty propagation through multi-component computational pipelines from diagnostic posterior to AquaCrop parameter posterior to counterfactual simulation output; probabilistic validation methods including prediction-interval coverage assessment; and geospatial computing at scale. The responsible use of generative AI is explicitly addressed as a required component of this dimension (SR-03). In the specific context of this project, responsible use of generative AI covers the limitations of AI-generated code in decision-critical agricultural modelling, where a model implementation error propagates into a physically incorrect irrigation recommendation; the need for human interpretability and validation of probabilistic model outputs before any uncertainty-weighted recommendation is presented to a farm manager or end user; the ethical dimensions of AI-assisted scientific writing under the research integrity requirements governing peer-reviewed publication; and the correct representation of AI-tool use in the project's open-science documentation. The project's methodological identity — honest uncertainty — is itself a form of responsible AI practice: a framework designed to report what it does not know as explicitly as what it knows operationalises responsible AI at the level of the system design, making responsible AI training integrated with the research rather than appended as a compliance module.

**Knowledge valorisation and innovation/entrepreneurship.** The project produces a standalone research demonstrator (OBJ-4, D2-01, Decision engine and demonstrator software, due month 20) at TRL 3–4 — a working end-to-end uncertainty-aware irrigation decision pipeline. Training in knowledge valorisation and innovation/entrepreneurship covers: the pathway from this research prototype toward potential future uptake, including intellectual property considerations for open research software produced under the MSCA-PF framework; valorisation routes for agricultural decision tools in the Hungarian horticultural market, informed by AgroVIR's role as the project's farmer-access partner; stakeholder engagement practices for translating technical research results into a form accessible to the end-user community (Hungarian tomato growers); and the strategic reasoning behind the TRL 3–4 positioning — understanding why a research fellowship is not the appropriate vehicle for operational product development and what the pathway from TRL 3–4 to operational deployment would require. The mentorship for this dimension is provided directly by Dr. András Jung as co-founder of Cubert GmbH and Brightic Research, supplying supervision grounded in the first-hand experience of taking spectroscopic research from academic proof-of-concept to commercial instrument manufacturing — the most directly relevant entrepreneurship mentorship available for a fellow developing an uncertainty-aware agricultural sensing tool at TRL 3–4.

**Research integrity and good scientific conduct.** The project's own validation design is structured as a research integrity intervention. The validation protocol of WP3 (Field validation, ground truth and uncertainty calibration) pre-specifies all evaluation criteria before data collection begins: the ground-truth measurement protocol (RQ4: chlorophyll fluorescence, stomatal conductance, canopy and leaf temperature, spectral reflectance), the uncertainty-calibration criterion (RQ5: 90% prediction intervals must contain the measured ground-truth value approximately 90% of the time on the holdout validation set), the pilot-success definition (RQ7: the RQ5 calibration target is met on the holdout set), and the Route B patch-covariation test (RQ9: a co-located seasonal campaign demonstrating statistical covariation between the proxy patch and the target tomato crop) are all fixed before any field data are collected. This pre-specification is the project's operational form of research integrity in quantitative environmental modelling: by committing to evaluation criteria before observing the data, the project removes the possibility of post-hoc criterion adjustment to produce a favourable appearance of success, which is a documented failure mode in applied remote-sensing and agricultural modelling validation literature. Research integrity training in WP4 (Training, supervision and two-way transfer of knowledge) therefore engages with research integrity not as an abstract code of conduct but as a concrete methodological practice that the fellow is already implementing through the project's pre-specified validation design — an experience that is more educationally substantial than completing a generic research integrity course.

**Open science and FAIR data stewardship.** The project's open-science commitments are embedded in the deliverable structure. The Data Management Plan (D5-01, due month 6) formalises FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) principles for all data categories the project produces: the multi-sensor Earth-observation time series assembled and quality-controlled in WP1; the non-destructive physiological ground-truth measurements collected in WP3; the probabilistic model artefacts from WP1 and WP2; and the validation assessment outputs from WP3. The Dissemination and exploitation plan (D5-02, due month 6) commits the project to open-access publication of its scientific results and establishes the dissemination strategy at the beginning of the fellowship. Open research software publication — making the diagnostic branch software and the uncertainty-aware decision engine available under an open licence alongside the field-validation dataset and protocol — is a committed output. Training in this dimension covers both the practical skills of FAIR data management (metadata standards, repository platforms, data lifecycle planning, version control and provenance for model artefacts) and the scientific reasoning for open science in the specific context of probabilistic environmental modelling: a framework that claims to produce well-calibrated uncertainty cannot be independently verified if its code and data are not inspectable by third parties, which makes open-science practice scientifically necessary rather than merely programmatically required. The Milestone MS1 (Multi-sensor data pipeline operational; Data Management Plan delivered, due month 6) confirms that data management infrastructure and open-science planning are established at the beginning of the project's intensive data-collection phase.

**Feedback of research results into teaching and education.** As part of the fellowship's engagement with the host institution's academic environment, the transfer of plant-physiology and non-destructive measurement methods to the host group's researchers — through methods seminars and knowledge exchange events planned in WP4 (Training, supervision and two-way transfer of knowledge) — constitutes a form of intra-group research-into-teaching feedback. The specific format, frequency, and outputs of these knowledge exchange activities are planned in the Career Development Plan (D4-01, due month 3) rather than pre-specified here. These activities serve expected outcome EO-08 (regular feedback of research results into teaching and education at participating organisations) through the embedding of the fellowship's methodology in the host group's collective scientific practice and, via the host's educational activities, in its training of the next generation of geoinformatics and crop-science researchers.

### Non-academic placement and secondment

This proposal does not include a secondment phase or a non-academic sector placement within the 24-month European Fellowship. The evaluator is therefore not required to assess a rationale and added value for either mechanism.

AgroVIR (Hungary) participates in the project as an associated partner in a specific and confirmed role: providing access to Hungarian tomato farms for the field validation data collection and ground-truth measurement campaigns of WP3 (Field validation, ground truth and uncertainty calibration), and providing farmer feedback on the project's research demonstrator. This role is explicitly not structured as a formal secondment to AgroVIR as a host organisation and not as a formal non-academic sector placement: AgroVIR's confirmed role is farmer access, field validation, and feedback at Hungarian tomato sites, and its participation does not involve the fellow being employed by or formally placed at AgroVIR for any period. The concept refinement phase output confirms that AgroVIR is an associated partner for field access, not a secondment or placement host (CC-07 and CC-09, both marked not applicable in the project's scope coverage record). The optional secondment mechanism (scope requirement SR-05: for European Fellowships, not exceeding half the requested duration) has not been invoked because the project's scientific design does not require a formal period of work at a second host organisation to achieve its objectives. The optional non-academic sector placement mechanism (scope requirement SR-06: up to 6 months at a non-academic organisation established in an EU Member State or Horizon Europe Associated Country at the end of the project) has likewise not been invoked; the project's innovation and entrepreneurship training dimension is delivered through Dr. András Jung's supervision, grounded in his co-founding experience with Cubert GmbH and Brightic Research, rather than through a formal placement period at an external non-academic entity. Both decisions are consistent with the project's scientific design and with the 24-month European Fellowship duration, within which a formal secondment or placement would reduce the time available for the core research and training activities that make the fellowship scientifically coherent.
