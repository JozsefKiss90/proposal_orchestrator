# 1. Excellence

Excellence carries 50% of the total evaluation score for MSCA Postdoctoral Fellowships and is assessed across four sub-sections: the quality and pertinence of the R&I objectives and the degree to which the proposal goes beyond the state of the art (1.1); the soundness of the methodology (1.2); the quality of the supervision, training and two-way transfer of knowledge (1.3); and the excellence of the researcher's professional experience (1.4). The present application is a 24-month European Fellowship hosted at Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest, Hungary — Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics — supervised by Dr. András Jung, and undertaken by Dr. Rositsa Cholakova, a plant physiologist currently based at the Maritsa Vegetable Crops Research Institute, Bulgarian Agricultural Academy, Plovdiv, Bulgaria. The scientific content concerns uncertainty-aware, multi-sensor crop water stress diagnosis and water-use-efficient irrigation decision support for tomato on Hungarian farms.

---

## 1.1 Quality and pertinence of the R&I objectives; going beyond the state of the art

### The scientific problem and its pertinence

Irrigation decisions for row crops must be made under two compounding layers of uncertainty. The first is epistemic: the true state of the field — how much water is in the root zone, and how stressed the crop already is — is only partially and noisily observable from the combination of ground sensors, satellite imagery, and meteorological data that are practically accessible to a farm or research installation. The second is aleatory: even if the current state were known perfectly, the consequence of each irrigation choice depends on future weather that has not yet occurred. Together these uncertainties mean that any operationally useful irrigation decision support system must do three things that current systems typically do poorly in combination: (i) diagnose the latent field state from multiple uncertain data streams with an honest quantification of that uncertainty; (ii) simulate forward under multiple plausible future weather scenarios to score alternative irrigation decisions; and (iii) recommend the decision that best achieves an agronomic or economic criterion under that forward uncertainty, rather than suppressing the uncertainty and returning an overconfident point recommendation.

The proposed project addresses all three components in a single coherent framework, applied to tomato (operator-confirmed, RQ1) grown on Hungarian farms accessed via the associated partner AgroVIR. The crop choice is scientifically motivated: tomato is both highly sensitive to over- and under-irrigation and an economically significant crop in Hungarian horticulture, and it is the crop on which Dr. Rositsa Cholakova's doctoral thesis focussed ("Non-destructive evaluation of physiological status of drought-stressed tomato plants", PhD summa cum laude, 2020, doi:10.2139/ssrn.5492527), making the fellow the project's biological ground-truth authority for the exact crop-stress domain the project investigates.

The call HORIZON-MSCA-2026-PF-01 requires excellent researchers undertaking international mobility. This project satisfies both defining features: Dr. Cholakova is a confirmed experienced researcher (PhD 2020, approximately four years post-doctoral) undertaking an intra-EU European Fellowship from Plovdiv, Bulgaria to Budapest, Hungary, consistent with scope requirement SR-01. The research domain — crop water stress monitoring and irrigation decision support — addresses European food security, agricultural water efficiency, and the sustainability goals to which the European Research Area contributes. The project directly serves the call's expected outcome EO-07 (stronger R&I capacity and output among participating organisations; better transfer of knowledge) through the genuine two-way disciplinary exchange between plant physiology and geoinformatics/remote sensing expertise.

### Going beyond the state of the art

The state-of-the-art review underlying this proposal is grounded in a systematic survey of the literature on multi-sensor crop water stress monitoring. The review's research-gap matrix charts the density of published work across two gradients: depth of sensor integration (single-sensor to multi-modal) and task complexity (stress detection to quantification to optimisation to real-time decision support). Work density falls sharply as both gradients increase, and the two gradients meet in a single substantially empty region — real-time decision support combining PlanetScope high-resolution imagery with the broader multi-sensor stack. Genuine probabilistic precedents exist and are explicitly acknowledged: Bayesian Maximum Entropy approaches for multi-source drought fusion; copula-based scheduling linking drought indices to irrigation decisions; dynamic Bayesian networks for tracking field state; and multi-modal fusion network architectures. Each of these works advances one of the three components. None couples all three — learned multimodal fusion, explicit probabilistic crop-water-state estimation, and agronomically scored irrigation decision optimisation — end to end, with uncertainty carried honestly from observation through to the decision output.

The novelty claim is deliberately narrow and must be read as such by the evaluator. It is not "using satellites for irrigation," a crowded field with a long history. It is the end-to-end coupling of three specific capabilities that the reviewed literature reports as underdeveloped in combination: (i) learned probabilistic fusion of multi-source Earth observation — Sentinel-1 SAR radar backscatter, Sentinel-2 optical reflectance, and PlanetScope high-resolution imagery (access confirmed via Planet platform and API, RQ8) — with ERA5 meteorological reanalysis and field observations; (ii) explicit probabilistic estimation of the latent field state, producing a posterior distribution over root-zone soil moisture and crop water status (RQ3) rather than a point estimate; and (iii) water-use-efficiency-oriented irrigation decision optimisation (RQ6, decision objective operator-confirmed) via uncertainty-aware crop-model calibration and counterfactual simulation over weather ensembles. The claim is defensible only if the uncertainty chain is kept honest from end to end, and the entire methodological architecture is designed around that requirement. The proposal is framed as a research prototype at TRL 3–4 (RQ10, operator-confirmed), appropriate to a 24-month fellowship; overstating the scope or TRL would undermine the epistemic credibility that is itself the proposal's methodological identity.

### The five R&I objectives

The project is structured around five operator-confirmed objectives, each mapped to a work package in the 24-month work plan.

**Objective OBJ-1 — Probabilistic multi-sensor diagnosis of the latent field state.** Develop and calibrate a probabilistic diagnostic method that fuses multi-source Earth observation (Sentinel-1 SAR, Sentinel-2 optical, and secured PlanetScope imagery, RQ8) with meteorological data (ERA5 reanalysis) and field observations, producing estimates of the latent field state — root-zone soil moisture and crop water status (RQ3) — as a calibrated posterior distribution with explicit, honest uncertainty (posterior/prediction intervals) rather than a point estimate. The measurable target is uncertainty-calibrated field-state estimation; no fabricated accuracy percentage is asserted, because doing so would misrepresent the calibration objective. The primary implementation path is Route B — the homogeneous patch proxy (RQ2, operator-confirmed) — within a methodological architecture that also specifies Route A (direct one-step probabilistic fusion) as a rigorous alternative implemented for comparison and fallback purposes (see 1.2 below). Addressed in WP1 (Probabilistic multi-sensor diagnosis of the field state), with deliverable D1-01 (Diagnostic model specification and calibration report, due month 14).

**Objective OBJ-2 — Uncertainty-aware decision engine for water-use-efficient irrigation choice.** Develop the prognostic decision engine: uncertainty-aware AquaCrop calibration that retains an honest parameter posterior rather than collapsing to a single best-fit parameter set; counterfactual irrigation simulation over weather ensembles; and scoring of candidate irrigation decisions by water-use efficiency (WUE) under weather and model uncertainty (RQ6, decision objective operator-confirmed). The decision engine explicitly propagates uncertainty from OBJ-1's diagnostic branch through to the irrigation recommendation. The measurable target is an integrated engine that scores candidate decisions by WUE under combined weather and model uncertainty, propagating uncertainty from diagnosis into the recommendation. Addressed in WP2 (Uncertainty-aware decision engine and demonstrator integration), with deliverable D2-01 (Decision engine and demonstrator software, due month 20).

**Objective OBJ-3 — Field validation and honest uncertainty calibration.** Design and execute a validation protocol on Hungarian tomato farms accessed via AgroVIR: collect non-destructive physiological ground truth (RQ4: chlorophyll fluorescence, stomatal conductance, canopy/leaf temperature, spectral reflectance); assess whether 90% prediction intervals contain the true measured value approximately 90% of the time (the uncertainty-calibration criterion, RQ5); define pilot success as that calibration target being met on the validation set (RQ7); and test Route B's patch-covariation assumption via a co-located seasonal campaign measuring the target tomato crop and the proxy patch simultaneously and demonstrating statistical covariation (RQ9). No validation outcome numbers beyond these operator-confirmed criteria are asserted. Addressed in WP3 (Field validation, ground truth and uncertainty calibration), with deliverable D3-01 (Field validation dataset and uncertainty calibration report, due month 24).

**Objective OBJ-4 — Standalone research demonstrator (web MVP).** Integrate the diagnostic branch (OBJ-1) and the decision engine (OBJ-2) into a standalone research demonstrator — a working end-to-end pipeline from multi-sensor observation to uncertainty-weighted irrigation recommendation — at TRL 3–4 (RQ10, operator-confirmed). The demonstrator is explicitly presented as a research artefact, not an operational product or commercial tool. The measurable target is a working end-to-end demonstrator that exercises the full observation-to-decision pipeline. Addressed in WP2 as the integration sub-task of T2-03, with deliverable D2-01.

**Objective OBJ-5 — Advancement of the fellow's competences and career.** Enhance Dr. Rositsa Cholakova's scientific, technical and transferable competences and career perspectives through the fellowship's training, supervision, and two-way transfer of knowledge (MSCA-PF Excellence criteria 1.3/1.4). The measurable target is: a Career Development Plan prepared jointly by supervisor and fellow and submitted as a project deliverable at the start of the action (D4-01, month 3), then reviewed and updated during the fellowship; structured transferable-skills training covering the MSCA-PF mandatory dimensions (digital and AI skills including responsible use of generative AI; knowledge valorisation; innovation and entrepreneurship; research integrity; and open science/FAIR data stewardship); peer-reviewed publication and conference dissemination of the project's results; and two-way transfer of knowledge evidenced by methods exchange between fellow and host group. Specific quantitative training and publication targets are not pre-asserted here; they will be determined jointly with the supervisor in the Career Development Plan, as the MSCA-PF instrument requires. Addressed in WP4 (Training, supervision and two-way transfer of knowledge) and WP5 (Dissemination, exploitation, communication and management).

## 1.2 Soundness of the methodology

### Methodological architecture: two routes, one honest uncertainty chain

The project's defining methodological commitment is the honest uncertainty chain: every model output must report not only the estimated field state but the uncertainty of that estimate, and that uncertainty must travel through the decision engine and emerge in the irrigation recommendation as an uncertainty-weighted score, never discarded at the crop-model interface. This is the characteristic that distinguishes the proposed framework from existing irrigation-support systems that return point estimates and from purely monitoring-oriented systems that stop at state detection without closing the loop to a decision.

The diagnostic branch that feeds this chain operates through two interchangeable routes, both designed to produce a posterior distribution over root-zone soil moisture and crop water status (RQ3) rather than a point estimate. They are architecturally coupled to the same downstream decision engine, so that a shift from one route to the other requires no change in the prognostic branch.

**Route A — Direct probabilistic fusion** is the rigorous one-step route. Multi-source Earth observation signals — Sentinel-1 SAR radar backscatter, Sentinel-2 optical surface reflectance, and secured PlanetScope high-resolution imagery (RQ8: access confirmed via Planet platform and API), together with ERA5 meteorological reanalysis and available field observations — enter a probabilistic machine-learning model jointly as covariates. The model maps the combined observation directly to the posterior distribution over the target field-state variables. Candidate model families, all returning distributions rather than point estimates, include Bayesian hierarchical models and Gaussian processes. The one-step design avoids the sub-pixel aggregation problem: coarse-resolution satellite data cannot reliably represent the sub-pixel heterogeneity of a crop field when disaggregated from pixel scale to field scale. By fusing all sensors at the model level rather than disaggregating intermediate pixel-scale estimates, Route A sidesteps a principal source of structural uncertainty inflation that affects two-step approaches that first estimate an intermediate vegetation index and then downscale it.

**Route B — Homogeneous patch proxy** is the lighter, resolution-stable route and the primary implementation path for this project (RQ2, operator-confirmed). It sidesteps the sub-pixel resolution problem by identifying a nearby homogeneous patch — matched on high-resolution soil and climate data to the target crop area — whose vegetation biology covaries with the target tomato crop and whose spatial homogeneity allows the coarse satellite pixel to read it cleanly without the need for spatial disaggregation. The coarse pixel reads the proxy patch cleanly; the inferred state of the proxy patch stands as a surrogate for the latent field state of the target crop. Route B also includes the Control Stand Variant: deliberately planting a covarying species as a designed proxy sensor so that the patch-covariation relationship can be measured through the growing season rather than assumed from spatial matching alone.

**The design is risk-managed by construction.** Route B is the committed primary method (RQ2) and Route A defines the methodological aspiration for a one-step rigorous alternative. Both routes feed the same downstream decision engine. If Route B's patch-covariation assumption fails the formal experimental test (RQ9), Route A provides the fallback without requiring a redesign of the prognostic branch; the project can shift to the rigorous route while preserving the validated decision engine. The conditional fallback structure means that the project is not existentially dependent on a single architectural assumption. Route B's central assumption — that the proxy patch genuinely covaries with the target tomato crop at seasonal scale — is formally required to be proven rather than assumed, and the testing protocol is defined before data collection begins (RQ9).

### Data acquisition and harmonisation (WP1, Task T1-01)

The Earth-observation stack assembled for this project combines three satellite data streams of complementary sensitivity and spatial resolution:

- *Sentinel-1 SAR:* radar backscatter at C-band, sensitive to surface and shallow subsurface soil moisture conditions and canopy structure, open Copernicus data, all-weather acquisition.
- *Sentinel-2 optical:* multispectral surface reflectance at 10–20 m resolution, sensitive to vegetation greenness, chlorophyll content, and canopy water content, open Copernicus data.
- *PlanetScope:* high-resolution (3 m) daily optical imagery, enabling sub-field spatial resolution of crop heterogeneity; access is confirmed via the Planet platform and API (RQ8, operator-confirmed).
- *ERA5 meteorological reanalysis:* ECMWF global reanalysis providing temperature, precipitation, solar radiation, and evapotranspiration estimates at hourly resolution, essential for the prognostic branch's weather ensemble scenarios.
- *Field observations:* ground measurements from Hungarian tomato farms accessed via AgroVIR, providing in-situ calibration and validation data.

The combination of radar (soil-moisture sensitive), optical (vegetation-status sensitive), high-resolution (spatially resolved), and meteorological inputs is the multi-modal stack that characterises the end of the research-gap gradient identified in the state-of-the-art review. The confirmed access to PlanetScope (RQ8) removes the most common pre-proposal data-access risk for high-resolution agricultural monitoring projects.

Data harmonisation across sensors with different spatial resolution, temporal cadence, and acquisition geometry is the technical challenge addressed in Task T1-01. The harmonisation strategy is informed by the two-route architecture: Route B demands only that the proxy patch be readable at the native Sentinel-1/2 pixel scale, which is tractable; Route A's one-step fusion approach requires that all streams be jointly used as model covariates, which is mathematically well-posed for probabilistic models that treat each stream as a noisy measurement of the latent state.

### Diagnostic branch calibration (WP1, Tasks T1-02 and T1-03)

Route B implementation (T1-02) selects and monitors the homogeneous proxy patch: identifying candidate patches in the vicinity of AgroVIR's Hungarian tomato farms using high-resolution PlanetScope imagery and soil/climate ancillary data, establishing the co-located measurement campaign (a prerequisite for RQ9), and implementing the seasonal proxy-reading pipeline that transforms coarse-pixel satellite observations of the homogeneous patch into posterior estimates of the target field state. Route A implementation (T1-02) operationalises the one-step probabilistic fusion model for the same field state.

Calibration of the diagnostic branch (T1-03) produces calibrated posterior estimates of root-zone soil moisture and crop water status across the growing season. These posteriors are the primary output consumed by the prognostic branch (T2-01) and by the validation branch (T3-02), constituting the critical dependency edge on the project's dependency graph. T1-03's output is a prerequisite for both the uncertainty-aware AquaCrop calibration (T2-01) and the uncertainty-calibration quality assessment (T3-02); this dependency is the critical path of the project.

### Prognostic branch and decision engine (WP2)

From the diagnosed field state onward, both diagnostic routes use the same prognostic engine (WP2). The calibrated posterior from T1-03 is passed to the FAO AquaCrop crop model, which serves as the interface through which alternative irrigation decisions act on the diagnosed field state. AquaCrop calibration is performed in an uncertainty-aware way: rather than collapsing the parameter space to a single maximum-likelihood parameter set (a standard calibration failure mode that produces overconfident simulations), the calibration retains a distribution over AquaCrop parameters consistent with the observed field data. This uncertainty-aware parameter posterior propagates through the simulation.

Counterfactual irrigation simulations (T2-02) are then run over weather ensembles — multiple plausible realisations of future weather drawn from historical climatology or medium-range weather forecasts — for each candidate irrigation decision. The result is a distribution of simulated crop-water outcomes for each decision option, under joint uncertainty from both the field-state diagnosis and the future weather. The decision engine scores each candidate decision by its expected water-use efficiency (WUE, RQ6) under that joint distribution, and returns the recommendation with its associated uncertainty. The WUE objective is instantiated in the vault's objective-function machinery; the same machinery is architecturally open to profit-based or drought-risk-based objectives, but WUE is the committed instantiation for this project (RQ6).

The standalone research demonstrator (T2-03) integrates the diagnostic branch, the prognostic engine, and the decision interface into a working end-to-end pipeline presented as a web-based research prototype (TRL 3–4, RQ10). Deliverable D2-01 (Decision engine and demonstrator software, month 20) is the software artefact of WP2.

### Field validation and uncertainty calibration (WP3)

The validation protocol is defined in full before data collection begins. All criteria are operator-confirmed and none are post-hoc:

*Ground truth (RQ4).* Non-destructive physiological measurements are collected on Hungarian tomato plants at AgroVIR field sites: chlorophyll fluorescence (relating leaf photosystem II quantum efficiency to photosynthetic water stress); stomatal conductance (quantifying stomatal aperture as a direct measure of plant water status and stress response); canopy and leaf temperature (infrared thermometry capturing the canopy-air temperature difference that arises under water stress due to reduced transpirational cooling); and spectral reflectance (capturing vegetation indices and water-sensitive spectral bands at leaf and canopy scale). These measurements are non-invasive, plant-physiologically grounded, and directly related to the crop water stress mechanisms the project targets. They are the fellow's primary research competence and the project's biological validation strength.

*Uncertainty calibration criterion (RQ5).* The diagnostic branch's uncertainty claims are evaluated against a frequentist coverage criterion: 90% prediction intervals must contain the measured ground-truth value approximately 90% of the time. This is a necessary condition for the model's reported uncertainty to be meaningful. A model that produces narrow intervals that exclude the truth more than 10% of the time is overconfident and produces irrigation recommendations whose apparent precision is spurious. A model that produces wide intervals that always contain the truth is uninformatively conservative. The RQ5 criterion targets the calibration point: honest, appropriately narrow uncertainty.

*Pilot success criterion (RQ7).* Pilot success is defined as the RQ5 calibration target being met on the validation set — the holdout dataset of field measurements not used in calibration. This is the project's go/no-go criterion.

*Patch covariation test (RQ9).* Route B's structural assumption — that the homogeneous proxy patch genuinely covaries with the target tomato crop — is tested by a co-located seasonal measurement campaign in which the target crop and the proxy patch are measured simultaneously throughout at least one growing season, and the statistical covariation of their water-status signatures is demonstrated. Without this demonstration, Route B's inference chain cannot be defended.

Task T3-01 designs and runs the field validation protocol on Hungarian tomato farms. Task T3-02 assesses uncertainty calibration quality against the RQ5 criterion, consuming the diagnostic posterior from T1-03. Task T3-03 runs the co-located patch-covariation campaign to validate Route B's central assumption. Deliverable D3-01 (Field validation dataset and uncertainty calibration report, month 24) is the combined scientific output of WP3.

**Coherence caveat (noted transparently).** The RQ4 non-destructive physiological measurements — chlorophyll fluorescence, stomatal conductance, canopy/leaf temperature, spectral reflectance — validate the crop-water-status component of the target field state (RQ3) directly, because these measurements respond to crop physiological water stress. They validate root-zone soil moisture, the other component of RQ3, only indirectly: through the plant's water-relations response to soil moisture deficit. This is a methodological coherence caveat, not a gap; root-zone soil moisture influences crop water status through well-understood plant hydraulics, and the non-destructive measurements capture the downstream physiological consequences. No additional soil-moisture sensor instrument plan is fabricated beyond the multi-sensor fusion strategy of WP1.

### Open science and ethical considerations

The project's open-science commitment is embedded in the work plan rather than appended to it. The validation protocol is designed for reproducibility: measurement procedures, the RQ5 calibration criterion, and the RQ9 covariation campaign are pre-specified and fixed before data collection, precluding post-hoc criterion selection. The research software implementing the diagnostic branch and the decision engine will be published under an open licence alongside the validation dataset and protocol (D3-01). The Data Management Plan (D5-01, month 6) formalises FAIR principles for the multi-sensor time series, field measurement datasets, and model artefacts produced by the project. Open-access publication of results is committed through the Dissemination and exploitation plan (D5-02, month 6). Gender and diversity considerations are addressed in the Career Development Plan (D4-01, month 3) and governed by Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest's institutional commitments under the European Charter for Researchers.

## 1.3 Supervision, training and two-way transfer of knowledge

### The host institution

Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest, Hungary — Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics — is the host beneficiary. ELTE is Hungary's leading research university and a higher education establishment with deep institutional experience in hosting internationally mobile researchers. The Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics, concentrates expertise in geoinformatics and remote sensing, the methodological domain that supplies the project's Earth-observation data acquisition, multisensor fusion, and spatial data processing capabilities. The host group's geoinformatics and remote-sensing infrastructure and computing environment are the host-to-fellow side of the two-way transfer, providing the fellow with tools, methods, and scientific community access unavailable at her current plant-physiology institute in Plovdiv.

### The supervisor

Dr. András Jung is the supervisor, serving as Deputy Head of the Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics at ELTE Faculty of Informatics. His expertise spans remote sensing, hyperspectral imaging and field spectroscopy, and multisensor-drone fusion. He is Head of the Multisensor-Drone-Fusion Working Group at ELTE (project ELTE TKP2021-NVA-29), which directly supplies the multi-sensor observation infrastructure relevant to the project's diagnostic branch. He is co-founder of Cubert GmbH (a manufacturer of hyperspectral snapshot cameras) and Brightic Research, demonstrating a track record of translating spectroscopic research methods from laboratory to commercial hardware — first-hand experience directly relevant to the project's knowledge-valorisation and innovation/entrepreneurship training dimension and to the demonstrator's pathway from TRL 3–4 toward eventual uptake. Dr. Jung holds habilitations in both Computer Science and Crop Sciences, a dual disciplinary qualification that uniquely positions him to supervise a project located at the intersection of geoinformatics and plant-crop science.

The supervisor's expertise is the complement of the fellow's: Dr. Jung provides the Earth-observation methodology and geospatial data science competence that the project's diagnostic branch requires, while Dr. Cholakova provides the plant-physiology and non-destructive measurement expertise that the project's validation protocol requires. The supervision pairing is scientifically motivated by this asymmetric complementarity.

### Supervision structure and Career Development Plan

Supervision is structured around the Career Development Plan (D4-01), jointly prepared by Dr. András Jung and Dr. Rositsa Cholakova and submitted as a project deliverable at the start of the action (month 3). Regular research reviews are conducted throughout the 24-month fellowship under Dr. Jung's supervision within the host's geoinformatics and remote-sensing group, enabling early identification of methodological risks, scientific progress assessment, and adaptive revision of priorities where the route-fallback design requires it.

### Two-way transfer of knowledge

The two-way transfer of knowledge between Dr. Rositsa Cholakova and Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest is genuine rather than formal — meaning that each direction of transfer addresses a real scientific need of the project that the receiving party cannot address from within its own existing expertise.

**Fellow to host.** Dr. Cholakova brings to the ELTE geoinformatics group a practitioner-level expertise in plant physiology and non-destructive evaluation of crop water and abiotic stress that the host group does not possess. This expertise is load-bearing for the project's ground-truth and validation tasks: the non-destructive physiological measurements specified in RQ4 — chlorophyll fluorescence, stomatal conductance, canopy/leaf temperature, and spectral reflectance — require a scientist who understands not only the measurement instruments but the biophysical interpretive framework that connects instrument readings to plant water-stress mechanisms. Without this competence, the project's validation protocol (OBJ-3) cannot be executed reliably. The fellow's doctoral thesis was specifically on non-destructive evaluation of drought-stressed tomato plants (doi:10.2139/ssrn.5492527), making her the specific domain authority for the exact crop-stress combination the project targets. The ELTE group permanently acquires, through the fellowship, the capacity to conduct biologically validated field campaigns for crop-water-stress monitoring — a capacity that did not previously exist in the group.

**Host to fellow.** The ELTE Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics transfers to the fellow the methods and working environment of a mature geoinformatics and remote-sensing research group: multi-sensor Earth-observation data acquisition and processing pipelines, hyperspectral imaging and field spectroscopy practices, spatial data harmonisation across satellite sensors of different spatial and temporal resolution, multisensor-drone fusion techniques, and geospatial data management infrastructure. These are the methodological tools on which the project's diagnostic branch (WP1, OBJ-1) is built. The fellow arrives from a plant-physiology institute that, while expert in its own domain, has not operated at the scale of satellite multi-sensor data processing that the project requires. By the end of the fellowship, the fellow will have built genuine competence in these geoinformatics methods, positioning her for independent research at the intersection of plant physiology, Earth observation, and probabilistic agricultural data science.

**Mutual irreplaceability.** The combined competence — plant physiology plus geoinformatics plus probabilistic machine learning — is what is required to execute the proposed end-to-end uncertainty-aware framework. Neither component alone is sufficient. The host cannot execute the validation without the fellow's physiological expertise; the fellow cannot execute the diagnostic branch without the host's EO infrastructure and supervision. This mutual dependence is the scientific justification for the specific researcher-host pairing.

### Transferable-skills training across MSCA-PF mandatory dimensions

WP4 (Training, supervision and two-way transfer of knowledge), led by Dr. András Jung, delivers structured training across all MSCA-PF mandatory transferable-skills dimensions specified in scope requirement SR-03. The specific quantitative targets for each dimension — hours of training, outputs produced, seminars delivered — will be determined jointly between supervisor and fellow in the Career Development Plan (D4-01, month 3) and are not pre-asserted here, as the CDP process is the appropriate instrument for setting those targets collaboratively and proportionally to the fellow's career stage and development needs.

The Data Management Plan (D5-01, month 6) trains the fellow in FAIR principles applied to multi-sensor time series, field measurement datasets, and model artefacts. Open research software and open-access publications are committed outputs, embedding open-science practice in the project's deliverable structure rather than treating it as an afterthought.

## 1.4 The researcher's professional experience

### Identity and current position

The postdoctoral fellow is Dr. Rositsa Cholakova. Her current affiliation is the Maritsa Vegetable Crops Research Institute, Bulgarian Agricultural Academy, Plovdiv, Bulgaria (prior affiliation: Agricultural University - Plovdiv). She is approximately four years post-doctoral, having completed her PhD summa cum laude in 2020. The MSCA mobility rule (scope requirement SR-01) is satisfied: Dr. Cholakova moves from Plovdiv, Bulgaria to Budapest, Hungary, an intra-EU European Fellowship. She has not been a resident of and has not had her principal activity in Hungary for the twelve months prior to the application deadline.

### Doctoral formation and PhD-level expertise

Dr. Cholakova's PhD thesis — "Non-destructive evaluation of physiological status of drought-stressed tomato plants" (summa cum laude, 2020, doi:10.2139/ssrn.5492527) — is directly load-bearing for the proposed project in a way that is unusual and worth stating explicitly to the evaluator. The thesis subject is not adjacent to the project or merely background: it is the ground-truth measurement task that the project's validation protocol (OBJ-3, WP3, RQ4) is built around. The thesis establishes that Dr. Cholakova has hands-on expertise with chlorophyll fluorescence measurements, stomatal conductance measurements, canopy and leaf temperature measurements by infrared thermometry, and spectral reflectance — the exact four measurement types specified in RQ4 as the project's non-destructive physiological ground truth. The coincidence of the fellow's doctoral expertise and the project's required competence is not fortuitous; the project's ground-truth design was shaped by the fellow's established capabilities, which gives the validation protocol a credibility grounded in demonstrated track record rather than asserted capability.

### Research expertise and breadth

Dr. Cholakova's expertise covers: plant physiology; non-destructive techniques for the evaluation of abiotic and biotic plant stress and crop monitoring; photosynthesis under environmental stress; and biostimulants. Her peer-reviewed publication record includes further work on maize chilling-stress photosynthesis (extending her crop-stress physiological expertise to a second major row crop) and biostimulants (extending her plant-physiology competences into stress mitigation). This breadth means that her competences are not narrowly confined to the tomato-drought-stress domain of her thesis; the plant-physiological measurement skills, the non-destructive evaluation methodology, and the stress-physiology interpretive framework transfer across crop species and stress types.

### Scientific fit with the host group and supervisor

The scientific justification for Dr. Cholakova's placement at Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest — Faculty of Informatics, Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics — under Dr. András Jung is the complementarity of her expertise with the host group's, and the necessity of that complementarity for the project's execution.

Dr. Cholakova's expertise in plant physiology and non-destructive stress phenotyping addresses the biological and physiological component of the project's end-to-end framework. It enables the ground-truth validation (OBJ-3) and informs the choice and interpretation of the non-destructive measurements used to characterise crop water status. Her expertise does not, prior to the fellowship, extend to large-scale satellite EO data processing, probabilistic geospatial modelling, or the geoinformatics infrastructure needed for the diagnostic branch (OBJ-1). This is the competence gap that the fellowship is designed to address — not a weakness of the application, but the rationale for the mobility.

Conversely, the ELTE Institute of Cartography and Geoinformatics under Dr. Jung brings the multi-sensor EO methodology, hyperspectral imaging and field spectroscopy infrastructure, and probabilistic geospatial modelling expertise that the diagnostic branch requires. The host group does not, prior to the fellowship, have practitioner-level expertise in the plant-physiological measurement methods needed for the project's biological ground truth. This is what the fellow brings to the host.

The combination is necessary for the project, unavailable within either expertise base alone, and mutually beneficial: the project advances the science it claims to advance, and both parties emerge with capabilities they did not previously possess. This is the scientific logic of the researcher-host pairing, and it is grounded in the operator-confirmed identities of the fellow (Dr. Rositsa Cholakova, plant physiologist, Maritsa Vegetable Crops Research Institute, Bulgarian Agricultural Academy) and the host (Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), Budapest, geoinformatics and remote sensing, supervised by Dr. András Jung).

### Career development trajectory

Dr. Cholakova is at a career inflection point: approximately four years post-doctoral, with an established expertise in plant physiology and non-destructive stress evaluation, she is seeking to extend her research scope into the Earth-observation and data-science methods that are increasingly central to large-scale crop monitoring and agricultural decision support. The fellowship at ELTE addresses precisely this extension, pairing her existing plant-physiological competences with the geoinformatics and probabilistic machine-learning methods she needs to work independently at the frontier of Earth-observation-based crop science. The end-to-end uncertainty-aware framework she will develop and validate — from multi-sensor observation to probabilistic diagnosis to agronomic decision — defines a research competence space that is currently undersupplied and in growing demand for food-security, climate-adaptation, and water-efficiency research in Europe and globally.

The Career Development Plan (D4-01, submitted at month 3), prepared jointly with Dr. András Jung, will specify the structured pathway through which the fellowship advances Dr. Cholakova toward research independence at this disciplinary intersection: research milestones, publication planning, transferable-skills training targets across the MSCA-PF mandatory dimensions, and a strategy for the post-fellowship period. The CDP is not a formality; it is the governing instrument for the fellow's professional development throughout the 24-month European Fellowship.
