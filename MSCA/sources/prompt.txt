In one sentence: the project would diagnose crop/soil/water status from multiple uncertain data streams, then use that diagnosis to simulate alternative irrigation decisions and recommend the option with the best expected economic outcome.

The “red thread” is uncertainty: every model output should say not only “what is the state?” but also “how sure are we?”, and that uncertainty must travel all the way into the irrigation decision.

1. The core architecture

A proposal-friendly version of the method looks like this:

Satellite + soil sensors + weather + field observations → probabilistic field-state diagnosis → uncertainty-aware AquaCrop calibration → counterfactual irrigation simulations → profit/risk-based recommendation

So there are two scientific blocks:

Block	Plain meaning	Scientific role
Diagnostic block	What is happening now in the crop/soil system?	Estimate water stress, soil moisture, crop condition, phenology, etc.
Prognostic block	What will happen if we choose irrigation option A, B, C?	Run future scenarios and select the best decision.

The literature review supports this framing: many studies combine Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, weather and/or soil sensors, but full integration with PlanetScope, soil sensors, meteorology and operational irrigation decision support is still rare. The appended note in the review makes the even sharper point: “fusion → probabilistic drought state → optimized irrigation” is not yet well established.

2. Why satellite data are difficult here

Your colleague’s key concern is the sub-pixel problem.

A satellite pixel is not always the same size as the real field plot. For small horticultural plots, one pixel may contain the target crop plus neighbouring vegetation, bare soil, roads, buildings, or other fields. So the satellite does not “see” the plot cleanly.

That is why people use downscaling: they try to turn a coarse satellite observation into a finer-resolution map using statistical or machine-learning models. Spatial downscaling of satellite products is a known research area, especially where original satellite resolution is too coarse for field-level use.

Your colleague is saying: do not first manufacture a fine pixel and then use that fine pixel as if it were a real measurement. That creates a two-step error chain:

covariates → estimated fine pixel → crop/water-stress prediction

The proposed alternative is:

covariates + uncertainty → crop/water-stress prediction directly

That is a stronger methodological argument, because it avoids pretending that an estimated pixel is an observed fact.

3. Route 1: “Hardcore” probabilistic fusion model

This is the more scientific / ML-heavy route.

The model would take all available data directly:

Sentinel-1 radar data: useful because it works through cloud cover and is sensitive to surface structure/moisture.
Sentinel-2 optical data: vegetation indices such as NDVI, NDWI, NDMI.
PlanetScope imagery: higher spatial resolution, useful for small fields if available.
Weather data: rainfall, temperature, evapotranspiration, radiation, wind.
Soil sensors: in situ soil moisture, temperature, possibly conductivity.
Field observations: crop type, growth stage, irrigation logs, yield, biomass, visual stress observations.

Then it estimates the hidden state of the field: root-zone soil moisture, plant water stress, canopy development, biomass, expected yield, etc.

The models your colleague mentions:

Bayesian hierarchical model: a statistical model that estimates both field-level effects and general crop/soil/weather relationships, while explicitly producing uncertainty ranges.
Gaussian process: a probabilistic model especially useful for spatial or spatio-temporal prediction; it can estimate a value and an uncertainty surface.
Probabilistic machine learning: any ML approach that returns distributions or confidence intervals, not just point predictions.

This route is rigorous but demanding. It needs strong modelling expertise, good validation data, and careful computational design.

4. Route 2: the “patch” or biological proxy idea

This is the cleverer, lighter route.

Instead of trying to force coarse satellite pixels to describe a tiny horticultural plot, the project identifies a nearby homogeneous patch that the satellite can observe cleanly. The patch should have similar soil, climate and biological response to the target crop.

The logic is:

“If the target tomato plot is too small/noisy for satellite observation, find or create a nearby biological proxy that reacts to water stress in a correlated way and is large/homogeneous enough for the satellite to read.”

The more experimental version is to plant a control stand of a covarying species. That would turn the patch into a designed proxy sensor.

This is attractive for a proposal because it is scientifically creative and easier to pilot. It also connects well to ecosystem continuity, semi-natural patches, extensive agriculture, and landscape-level sustainability.

But it needs validation. The proposal must show how the project will test whether the patch really covaries with the target crop.

5. AquaCrop’s role

AquaCrop is the bridge from monitoring to decision-making.

FAO describes AquaCrop as a crop growth model that simulates yield response to water and is especially suited to situations where water is a limiting factor.

In the proposed methodology, AquaCrop does not replace the remote-sensing model. It takes the diagnosed field state and asks:

What if we irrigate today?
What if we delay irrigation?
What if we apply less water?
What if water prices or input costs are high?
What if the coming weather is dry/wet/hot?

So AquaCrop becomes the decision simulator.

The colleague’s DOI appears to refer to a “Conditional interval reduction method” for optimizing process-based models, i.e. a way to calibrate a model while retaining parameter uncertainty rather than collapsing everything into one overconfident parameter set.

For proposal purposes, you can describe this as uncertainty-aware calibration of AquaCrop.

6. The decision principle: counterfactual optimization

The prognostic part is counterfactual:

“Given the current estimated field state, what would happen under each possible decision?”

Candidate decisions could be:

no irrigation;
irrigate today;
irrigate in two days;
apply 10 mm / 20 mm / 30 mm water;
deficit irrigation;
later, different fertilization strategies.

The model runs each candidate decision under multiple possible weather futures. Then it compares expected outcomes.

Importantly, your colleague says the objective should be profit, not yield. That is a strong proposal point. The system should optimize:

expected crop value - water cost - energy cost - input cost - operational cost

This moves the project from “nice monitoring dashboard” to “decision-support tool that can pay for itself.”

7. Key terminology in plain English
Data fusion: combining different data sources into one model.
Multimodal fusion: combining different types of signals, e.g. radar, optical imagery, sensors, weather.
Ground truth: real field measurements used to train or validate the model.
Covariate: an explanatory input variable, such as NDVI, rainfall, soil type, temperature.
Downscaling: estimating finer-resolution information from coarse-resolution data.
Cokriging: a geostatistical interpolation method that uses correlated auxiliary variables.
Latent state: the real but not directly observed condition of the crop/soil system.
Posterior distribution: the model’s updated uncertainty range after seeing the data.
Uncertainty propagation: carrying uncertainty from input data through all model steps to the final decision.
Counterfactual: a “what would happen if…” scenario.
Weather ensemble: multiple plausible future weather paths.
Expected utility / expected profit: average value of a decision across uncertain futures.
Decision regret: how much worse a chosen decision was compared with the best possible decision after the fact.
8. Infrastructure implied by the methodology

For the proposal, the infrastructure can be described in layers:

Layer	Components
Earth observation	Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, PlanetScope, field boundaries, cloud masking, temporal compositing
Ground sensing	Soil moisture sensors, weather station/meteo data, irrigation logs, crop observations
Data platform	Geospatial database, time-series database, metadata catalogue, quality-control pipeline
Modelling layer	Probabilistic fusion model, uncertainty estimation, AquaCrop calibration, scenario simulation
Decision layer	Irrigation recommendation engine, economic objective function, risk thresholds
Validation layer	Field trials, sensor comparison, model accuracy, uncertainty calibration, decision-performance metrics
User interface	Web MVP/dashboard for farmers/advisors/validation partners

For an MSCA-style proposal, you can present the MVP as a standalone research demonstrator, with AgroVIR-like partners helping with feedback, farmer access, testing and validation rather than owning or operating the tool.

9. Where the novelty probably sits

The novelty is not simply “using satellite data for irrigation.” That is too broad and already well covered.

The stronger novelty claim is:

“The project develops and validates an uncertainty-aware, end-to-end decision framework that directly fuses multi-source satellite, soil, weather and field data into probabilistic crop-water-state estimates, couples these estimates with AquaCrop-based counterfactual simulation, and optimizes irrigation decisions according to expected economic return under weather and model uncertainty.”

Even shorter:

“From multi-sensor observation to probabilistic diagnosis to economically optimized irrigation decision.”

That is the defendable edge.

10. What you should ask the scientific team to clarify

For proposal writing, I would ask them these questions:

Is the first crop definitely tomato, or more broadly horticultural row crops?
Will Route 1 and Route 2 both be implemented, or will one be the main method and the other a fallback/comparison?
What exactly is the target “field state”: soil moisture, crop water stress, yield risk, irrigation priority, or all of these?
What ground-truth measurements will be collected?
How will uncertainty be evaluated? For example: do 90% prediction intervals contain the true value about 90% of the time?
What is the decision objective: profit, water-use efficiency, yield stability, drought-risk reduction, or a weighted combination?
What will count as success in the pilot: better irrigation timing, water savings, profit increase, reduced stress detection error?
Is PlanetScope access secured or only optional?
How will the “patch” covariation be validated scientifically?
What is the TRL target: research prototype, validated MVP, or operational pilot?

My instinct: for the proposal, frame this as a two-route methodology with one shared decision engine. Route A is the rigorous probabilistic fusion route; Route B is the ecological proxy/control-patch route. Both feed the same uncertainty-aware AquaCrop counterfactual decision system. That gives the project scientific ambition, methodological flexibility, and a credible fallback if small-plot satellite resolution becomes too difficult.
