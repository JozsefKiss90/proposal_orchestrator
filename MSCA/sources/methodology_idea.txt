Now, to the idea itself: I like it a lot, so let me lay out how I would approach it. I'm putting the design down for you to build on, and I'll stay close as it takes shape. The problem really has two branches. The first is diagnostic: what is the actual state of the field, the plant, the soil, the water? The second is prognostic, with counterfactuals: given that state, what would happen under the different decisions, so that we can pick the best one. The two are tied together by one chain that carries its uncertainty from end to end, and keeping that uncertainty honest is, for me, the heart of the whole thing. The data itself - the ground truth and the signals - is the easy part to gather; the real work, where the project lives or dies, is turning it into a decision: the fusion, the modelling, the validation. The satellite is where it gets interesting, because it cannot be used as is. At today's resolutions a small horticultural plot is sub-pixel: the pixel sees mostly the surrounding area, not the plot itself. The obvious answer - almost too obvious - is downscaling, where a statistical layer (cokriging, deep learning) first turns the covariates into a fine pixel, and then a second model turns that pixel into the prediction. Cokriging does carry its own uncertainty, so that is not the issue; the issue is the two steps themselves. Covariates to pixel to prediction propagates the error multiplicatively - the second step inflating what the first already carries - and on top of it conditions on an estimated pixel as if it had been measured. There is simply no need to pass through the pixel: the covariates and their uncertainty can be used directly for the prediction, in one step. That single thread runs under both routes I'd like to show you. The first is the hardcore one. We feed the satellite simply as one more input into a probabilistic machine learning model - for example a Bayesian hierarchical model or a Gaussian process - that takes the satellite, the sensors, the soil and the weather together and goes straight to the field state with its uncertainty. One step instead of two, the covariates doing their work directly, with no intermediate pixel to inflate the error. It is rigorous, and it is the harder road, since it leans heavily on the machine learning side - the kind of thing that wants strong hands. The second is the creative and easier one - the patch idea - which sidesteps the resolution problem rather than solving it. We select a close, homogeneous patch, matched on our high-resolution soil and climate data, whose biology covariates with the chosen crop, so that similar conditions give similar effect; a clean patch like this the coarse pixel can read directly, with nothing to disaggregate. (One idea, just a seed for now: instead of only searching for such a patch, we could deliberately plant a control stand of a covarying species, which would hand us the homogeneity and let us measure the covariation instead of assuming it.) It is lighter to build, and depending on where we end up - the data we have, the landscape - it could turn out even better than the hardcore route. It has a nice side too: it leans on keeping natural, semi-natural patches in the landscape, which sits well with the continuity of ecosystems and with extensive agriculture, and the same trick scales there once horticulture has shown the way. From the diagnosed state onward the path is the same for both. It calibrates a simple AquaCrop model, which does two jobs at once: it stands for the plant, and it is the interface through which the decisions act. The calibration is uncertainty-aware, following a method I developed a few years ago (doi: 10.1016/j.envsoft.2022.105556), so that we keep an honest range rather than one overconfident parameter set. We would start with horticulture (do I remember right, that it would be tomato first?), and mainly with irrigation, which is exactly where AquaCrop is strong; fertilization can follow. Then, at each decision point, we run the model forward under all the candidate decisions, over weather ensembles, and steer toward the one with the best expected outcome at the end of the season - the objective being profit rather than yield, net of the water and input costs, since the whole point is that it should pay off. Up to my knowledge nobody has put exactly these pieces together quite like this, which is our real edge, if we defend it carefully.
